Salmon Nutrition Facts

Salmon provides a combination that is quite different from many other protein foods: it is high in protein while also containing a meaningful amount of fat .

The salmon represented on this page provides approximately 208 calories, 20g of protein, and 13g of fat per 100g , with virtually no carbohydrates.

That balance of protein and fat is an important part of understanding why salmon has more calories than very lean fish or skinless chicken breast.

Salmon Gets Calories From Both Protein and Fat

Salmon is a protein-rich food, but protein is not its only major source of calories.

A 100g portion contains around 20g of protein and 13g of fat . Fat provides more calories per gram than protein, so salmon's natural fat content contributes substantially to its total energy value.

This is why salmon can contain more calories per 100g than leaner seafood while still providing a significant amount of protein.

It also contains virtually no carbohydrates , so nearly all of its calories come from protein and fat.

What About Omega-3 Fatty Acids?

Salmon is one of the better-known food sources of the long-chain omega-3 fatty acids EPA and DHA .

The exact amount is not identical in every salmon. Species, diet, farming conditions, and whether the fish is wild or farmed can all affect its fatty-acid profile.

For this reason, I would avoid assigning one universal omega-3 number to every salmon entry unless the exact USDA food record provides it.

The important point is that salmon naturally provides EPA and DHA as part of its fat content.

Wild and Farmed Salmon Can Have Different Nutrition

“Salmon” does not describe one nutritionally identical food.

Atlantic salmon, sockeye, coho, chinook, and other varieties can differ in calories and fat content. Wild and farm-raised salmon can also have different nutrition profiles.

Farmed Atlantic salmon, for example, is often higher in total fat than some wild salmon varieties. This can make its calorie content higher per 100g.

So if another nutrition label gives you a different calorie number for salmon, that does not necessarily mean one source is wrong. You may simply be comparing different species or production methods.

Raw vs Cooked Salmon Matters Too

Cooking can change nutrition values per 100g because salmon loses water during preparation.

A 100g raw portion and a 100g cooked portion are therefore not automatically equivalent.

Baking, grilling, poaching, or pan-frying can also affect the final result differently. Added oil, butter, glaze, or sauce increases the calories of the finished meal beyond those naturally present in the salmon.

For accurate food tracking, use nutrition data that matches both the type of salmon and whether you weighed it raw or cooked .

How Portion Size Changes the Numbers

The figures above are based on 100g.

If your salmon fillet weighs 150g, it contains roughly one and a half times the nutrients listed for 100g, assuming it is the same type and preparation.

Using weight is more reliable than simply logging “one salmon fillet” because individual fillets can vary considerably in size.
