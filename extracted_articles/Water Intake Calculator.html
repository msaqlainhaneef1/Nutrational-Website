How Much Water Should You Drink Each Day?

Daily water needs are not the same for everyone. Your body size, physical activity, environment, diet, and how much you sweat can all influence how much fluid you need.

Use the Water Intake Calculator above to estimate your daily hydration target based on your weight, activity level, and climate. Your result is shown in liters, ounces, and 250 ml glasses so you can use whichever measurement is easiest for you.

Your Water Target Is a Starting Point

A calculated water target is best treated as a practical estimate rather than a strict number you must hit every day.

Your fluid needs can change from one day to the next. A quiet day indoors may require less fluid than a long workout or several hours spent outside in hot weather.

This is why the calculator considers more than body weight alone.

Body Weight

Larger bodies generally require more total fluid than smaller bodies, which is why weight is used as the starting point for the estimate.

Activity

Exercise and physical work increase fluid loss, especially when you sweat. More active days may therefore require more water than sedentary days.

Climate

Hot conditions can increase sweating, while spending time at higher altitudes may also affect hydration needs. Your environment matters when interpreting your result.

Water From Food and Other Drinks Counts Too

Your hydration does not come only from plain water.

Foods with a high water content, including fruits, vegetables, soups, and yogurt, contribute to your total water intake. Drinks such as milk, tea, and coffee also provide water.

That distinction is important because established daily water recommendations generally refer to total water from both foods and beverages , not only glasses of plain water.

So if your total daily water intake is several liters, you do not necessarily need to drink that entire amount as plain water.

How Do You Know If You Are Drinking Enough?

Rather than focusing on one exact number, pay attention to your normal hydration needs throughout the day.

Thirst can be a useful signal for many healthy adults. Urine color can also provide some context, although it is not a perfect hydration test. Urine is often lighter when fluid intake is higher and darker when it is lower, but foods, supplements, medications, and health conditions can also affect its color.

Your calculated target works best as a guide alongside these everyday signals.

Do You Need More Water When You Exercise?

Often, yes.

Exercise increases fluid losses through sweat, but the amount varies widely depending on workout duration, intensity, temperature, clothing, and individual sweat rate.

Because of this variation, there is no single extra amount of water that is ideal for every workout.

If you regularly train for long periods, exercise in extreme heat, or lose large amounts of sweat, your hydration and electrolyte needs may require more individualized planning.
