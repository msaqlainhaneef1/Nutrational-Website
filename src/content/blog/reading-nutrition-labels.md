## Why most people read labels wrong

Nutrition labels look simple. They list calories, macros, and a handful of vitamins, all in a tidy black and white box. But the way that information is presented is shaped by decades of food industry lobbying, and the result is a panel that quietly nudges you toward underestimating what you actually eat.

The single biggest trap is serving size. A package that looks like a single serving is often two or three servings on paper. A 20 ounce soda is technically 2.5 servings. A frozen burrito is often 2 servings. A bag of chips is rarely the 1 ounce the panel assumes. Most people glance at the calorie number and miss that they are about to eat two or three times that amount.

## The four numbers that actually matter

You do not need to track every line on the panel. Four numbers will tell you most of what you need to know about any packaged food.

### 1. Serving size in grams

Always check the serving size first, and compare it to how much you actually plan to eat. If the serving is 30 grams but your typical bowl is 60 grams, double every number on the panel. This is the step most people skip, and it is the reason calories always seem to add up faster than expected.

### 2. Calories per serving

Useful as a baseline, but only meaningful once you have done the serving size math. A 150 calorie snack eaten at twice the serving size is a 300 calorie snack, which is no longer a small treat.

### 3. Added sugars

The added sugars line is the most important addition to the label in decades. It separates the sugar that occurs naturally in a food (like the lactose in yogurt or the fructose in fruit) from the sugar the manufacturer added. The daily value for added sugar is 50 grams. A single flavored yogurt can easily hit 20 grams of that.

### 4. Sodium

Processed foods are the source of about 70 percent of the sodium in the average diet. Anything above 400 milligrams per serving is considered high sodium. Soups, frozen meals, and restaurant sauces are the worst offenders, often clocking in above 800 milligrams per serving.

## Common marketing tricks to ignore

Food packaging is allowed to make claims that sound meaningful but have no legal definition or a definition so loose it is meaningless. Here are the ones to ignore.

- "Natural" has no FDA definition. It does not mean organic, healthy, or unprocessed.
- "Made with whole grains" can mean the product is 99 percent refined flour with a pinch of whole wheat. Look for whole grain as the first ingredient instead.
- "No sugar added" does not mean low sugar. The product can still be packed with naturally occurring sugar from concentrated fruit juice.
- "Low fat" usually means the fat was replaced with sugar or starch to keep the product palatable. Check the added sugars line before assuming low fat means healthy.

## A 30 second label reading routine

With practice you can evaluate any packaged food in under 30 seconds. The routine goes like this.

1. Look at the serving size in grams. Compare to what you will actually eat.
2. Adjust calories per serving to your real portion.
3. Check added sugars against the 50 gram daily limit.
4. Check sodium against the 400 milligram per serving threshold.
5. Scan the first three ingredients on the ingredients list. If sugar, syrup, or a refined grain appears in the top three, the product is a treat, not a staple.

That is it. You do not need to track every vitamin or mineral on the panel. If you eat a reasonably varied diet with plenty of whole foods, the micronutrients take care of themselves.
