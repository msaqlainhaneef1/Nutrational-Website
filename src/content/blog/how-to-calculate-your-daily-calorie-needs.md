## Why calorie math is the foundation

Every diet, no matter how it is branded, comes down to energy balance. Eat more calories than you burn and you gain weight. Eat fewer and you lose. The specific foods you eat matter for health, satiety, and body composition, but the calorie equation is the gatekeeper. If you do not know roughly how many calories you burn in a day, you are flying blind.

This guide walks through the two numbers that matter most: your Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR) and your Total Daily Energy Expenditure (TDEE). Once you understand these, every nutrition decision becomes easier to evaluate.

## Step 1: Calculate your BMR

BMR is the number of calories your body would burn if you stayed in bed all day and did not move. It is the energy required to keep your heart beating, your lungs breathing, your brain thinking, and your cells maintaining themselves. For most sedentary people, BMR accounts for 60 to 70 percent of total daily energy expenditure.

The most accurate widely used BMR formula is Mifflin-St Jeor, published in 1990. It improved on the older Harris-Benedict equation, which tended to overestimate BMR by about 5 percent.

For men, the formula is:

```
BMR = 10 x weight (kg) + 6.25 x height (cm) - 5 x age + 5
```

For women:

```
BMR = 10 x weight (kg) + 6.25 x height (cm) - 5 x age - 161
```

The gender offset reflects differences in lean body mass at the same overall weight. Men typically carry more muscle, which burns more calories at rest than fat.

## Step 2: Multiply by an activity factor

BMR alone is not your daily calorie target. It is the floor. To estimate your actual daily burn, multiply BMR by an activity factor that reflects how much you move throughout the day. The result is your TDEE.

The standard activity multipliers are:

- Sedentary (desk job, no exercise, little walking): BMR x 1.2
- Lightly active (light exercise 1 to 3 days per week): BMR x 1.375
- Moderately active (exercise 3 to 5 days per week): BMR x 1.55
- Very active (hard exercise 6 to 7 days per week): BMR x 1.725
- Extra active (physical job or two a day training): BMR x 1.9

The biggest mistake people make with TDEE calculators is overestimating their activity level. If you work a desk job and do three 45 minute workouts per week, you are moderately active at best. Pick the lower option if you are unsure. You can always adjust upward after two weeks of data.

## Step 3: Adjust for your goal

Once you have your TDEE, adjusting for a goal is straightforward arithmetic.

For weight loss, subtract 500 calories per day to lose about 0.5 kilograms per week. This is the most widely recommended deficit because it produces visible progress while preserving muscle and minimizing hunger. A larger deficit of 750 to 1000 calories produces faster loss but is harder to sustain and carries more risk of muscle loss.

For muscle gain, add 200 to 400 calories per day. A small surplus supports muscle growth without excessive fat gain. Larger surpluses do not build muscle faster, they just add fat.

For maintenance, eat at your TDEE. Track your weight for two to three weeks. If it stays stable, your TDEE estimate was accurate. If it drifts up or down, adjust your intake by 100 to 200 calories and try again.

## Step 4: Recalculate periodically

TDEE is not a fixed number. It changes as your weight changes, as your activity changes, and as you age. A smaller body burns fewer calories, so a TDEE that worked at 85 kilograms will produce weight gain at 75 kilograms if not adjusted.

A good rule of thumb is to recalculate your TDEE every 5 to 10 kilograms of weight change, or every 6 to 12 months if your weight is stable. If your weight loss stalls for more than two weeks despite consistent tracking, the most likely cause is that your TDEE has dropped along with your weight.

## Why calculators are estimates, not gospel

Every TDEE calculator uses the same basic approach, but two people with identical inputs can have real TDEEs that differ by 200 to 500 calories. This happens because of differences in non-exercise activity thermogenesis, or NEAT. NEAT is the energy you spend walking, fidgeting, standing, and doing chores. It varies enormously between individuals, even those with similar workout routines.

This is why the standard advice is to use the calculator output as a starting estimate, then track your food intake and weight for two weeks, and adjust based on what actually happens. If the calculator says your TDEE is 2,400 calories and you maintain weight eating 2,100, your real TDEE is closer to 2,100. Trust the scale over the equation.

## Putting it all together

Here is a complete example. A 30 year old male, 178 cm tall, weighing 75 kg, working a desk job and exercising 4 days per week.

1. BMR = 10 x 75 + 6.25 x 178 - 5 x 30 + 5 = 750 + 1112.5 - 150 + 5 = 1717.5, rounded to 1718 calories.
2. Activity multiplier for moderately active: 1.55. TDEE = 1718 x 1.55 = 2663 calories.
3. For weight loss at 0.5 kg per week: target = 2663 - 500 = 2163 calories per day.
4. After two weeks of eating 2163 calories per day, if weight drops by about 1 kg, the estimate is accurate. If weight does not change, the real TDEE is lower than estimated. Reduce target by 200 calories and try again.

The math is simple. The discipline to track consistently and adjust based on real data is the hard part.
