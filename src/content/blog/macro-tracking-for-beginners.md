## What macros actually are

Macronutrients are the three calorie providing components of food: protein, carbohydrates, and fat. Your body needs all three in relatively large amounts to function. Alcohol is technically a fourth macro, but it is rarely tracked and contributes empty calories, so it is usually ignored in macro tracking.

Each macro plays a different role:

- Protein builds and repairs tissue, supports immune function, and signals fullness. It provides 4 calories per gram.
- Carbohydrates are the body's preferred energy source, fueling the brain, muscles, and central nervous system. Also 4 calories per gram.
- Fat supports hormone production, brain health, and the absorption of fat soluble vitamins. It provides 9 calories per gram, more than double the other two.

If you know your daily calorie target and your macro split, you can plan meals that hit both. This is more useful than calorie tracking alone because it controls not just how much you eat but what kind of weight you gain or lose.

## Setting your daily targets

The starting point is your TDEE, which you can calculate using our TDEE calculator. Once you have that number, pick a goal:

- For weight loss, subtract 500 calories from your TDEE.
- For maintenance, eat at your TDEE.
- For muscle gain, add 300 to 500 calories.

Then choose a macro split. The three most common approaches are:

- Balanced (30 percent protein, 40 percent carbs, 30 percent fat). Works for most people. Easy to sustain.
- High protein (40 percent protein, 35 percent carbs, 25 percent fat). Best for muscle preservation during weight loss or for people who train hard.
- Low carb (30 percent protein, 20 percent carbs, 50 percent fat). Useful for people who feel better on lower carb intakes or who specifically want to restrict carbohydrates.

If you have no strong preference, pick balanced. Use our macro calculator to do the math automatically.

## How to track without losing your mind

Macro tracking works best when it is boring and consistent. Here is a simple routine.

Pick a tracking app or use a spreadsheet. Enter everything you eat for at least two weeks. The first week is the hardest because you are learning portion sizes and looking up foods. By week two, you will recognize patterns and tracking will take five minutes per day.

Weigh foods raw when possible, since cooking changes water content and therefore the weight to calorie ratio. A 100 gram raw chicken breast becomes about 70 grams after cooking, but the calorie and protein content is the same. Tracking raw weight avoids this confusion.

Hit your protein target first. Protein is the macro most people under eat, and it has the biggest impact on body composition. If you hit your protein target and stay within your calorie budget, the carb to fat ratio matters less than you might think.

Be honest about cooking oils and condiments. A tablespoon of olive oil is 120 calories. A tablespoon of mayonnaise is 90. These add up fast and are easy to forget. Measure them.

## How long to track for

Most people benefit from 4 to 8 weeks of strict tracking to calibrate their sense of portion sizes and calorie density. After that, you can transition to a more intuitive approach where you eyeball portions and use the scale to confirm progress.

Return to strict tracking for a week or two whenever the scale stalls for more than 10 days. This usually reveals where extra calories have crept in. Common culprits are larger restaurant portions, untracked snacks, and cooking oils that have increased gradually.

Athletes preparing for competition, people with significant weight to lose, and people who feel out of control around food may benefit from longer periods of tracking. For everyone else, short bursts of tracking followed by intuitive eating tends to work better than indefinite tracking, which can become obsessive.

## Common pitfalls to avoid

Perfectionism is the biggest trap. A single meal that blows your macro targets does not ruin a week. Get back on track at the next meal rather than writing off the day and starting over tomorrow.

Ignoring vegetables is another common mistake. Vegetables are mostly water and fiber, so they contribute few calories, but they are essential for satiety, micronutrients, and digestive health. Aim for 300 to 500 grams of vegetables per day.

Tracking restaurant food is notoriously inaccurate. Restaurant portions can be 50 to 100 percent larger than what the menu states, and preparation methods vary. When you eat out, estimate as honestly as you can, accept that the number will be wrong, and adjust your other meals accordingly.

Finally, do not let tracking become your identity. Food is fuel, but it is also culture, social connection, and pleasure. The goal of tracking is to build awareness so you can make informed choices, not to turn every meal into a math problem.
